You are given every message one person sent in one conversation with an
assistant, in order, each with an index, and after each one what came back —
its opening, and its last piece where that says more.
Four things are asked of you: group the messages into tasks, say which of them
were not input at all, say which are about running the work rather than about
what is being made, and give every other message that was input the one column
it belongs to.

A task is **one thing being worked on**: one thing they were trying to get
made, settled or understood — something that, once it was done, they could look
at and say whether it is what they meant. It runs from first raising it through
every message it took, until they stopped. A message that works on the same
thing as the messages before it — asking about what came back, testing it,
taking it further, or adding to it — belongs to the same task, even when what
it asks for is new. A new task starts when the thing changes. Two things that
have nothing to do with each other are two tasks, however close together they
were raised.

A heading under which many such things sit is not a task. A heading names an
area that several things fall under; if it could not, on its own, be accepted
or rejected, it is a heading, and each thing raised, decided and finished under
it is a task of its own. Ask of every task you form:
is there one thing here that they could look at and accept or reject? If what
they would look at is several things that could each be right or wrong without
the others, it is several tasks.

**A message that answers what came back belongs to the task of the thing that
came back.** When a reply laid out a proposal, a set of options or a result,
and the message accepts it, picks one of the options, rejects it, or says to go
ahead with it, the message goes in the task of that proposal or result — even
when it adds nothing else. A proposal the assistant raised on its own is a task
of its own once they answer it, when it is about a different thing from the
task it arose in; about the same thing, it stays in that task. Read the reply's last piece for what was laid
out: that is what the next message answers.

Three things make this harder than it sounds:

**Tasks interleave.** They will be working on one subject, break off to report
something about another, and come back. Two subjects alternating across six
messages are two tasks, not one and not six. **Group by the thing worked on,
never by adjacency, and never by a heading.**

**An ending is rarely announced.** A task usually ends by their going quiet
about it rather than by their saying so, and either way what ends it is that
nothing more comes. Whether it ended well is not your question — it is decided
later, by the step that reads the whole task. Group; do not judge.

**Gaps prove nothing.** People come back to a thing hours or days later, and
one objective can run across several sittings before it is finished. Time
passing is not a task ending.

Every message must land in exactly one task. Nothing may be dropped, and
nothing may be in two. A message that pursues no objective goes into one task
marked `"objective": null` — messages about the exchange rather than about
anything being made, and messages that are a sound or a feeling with nothing
they are about. What decides
it is whether the message is after something, not what it is about.

That task carries `"reopened": false`, which means nothing for a task with no
objective; it exists to hold messages rather than to be read. Leave it out when
nothing belongs in it.

That is about what a message is for. The second job below is about something
else — whether a message was an attempt to say anything at all — and it is
asked of every message wherever it ended up. A message can answer both, and
nothing turns on that: a null task is set aside whole, so naming one of its
messages again costs nothing and loses nothing.

## Which messages were not input

Grouping is one job; this is the other, and it is asked separately because a
step asked only "where does this belong" will find somewhere for everything.

This is not a test of whether a message was any good. Almost everything anybody
sends is input, including the vague, the repetitive, the badly worded and the
already-said. All of that is theirs and all of it counts. What is picked out
here is the narrow case of something that was never an attempt to say anything
at all, and it has to be positively recognisable as that from the record rather
than merely unimpressive.

Two things in the record can show it, and either is enough on its own.

**The message is wholly a reaction.** Read it and what is there is a sound, an
exclamation, or a feeling with nothing it is about — somebody reacting, and
recognisably nothing else. This is not about a message being vague or thin
about what it wants. Anything that names something, points somewhere, or says
that something happened is an attempt to say something, however little of it
there is.

A verdict on what came back is one of those, even a bare one. Saying that it is
right, or that it is wrong, and stopping there is a small thing to say but it
is still saying it: somebody now knows what they made of what they were given,
and somebody who said nothing would have left them without that. Approval and
rejection are held to whatever the step that reads whole tasks holds them to,
and that is not this step's business.

**The reply shows what arrived was never read as words.** The other side asks
for the message again, or says it cannot make out what came through. That is
the record showing this was not what they meant to send, and it is the evidence
that decides. A message that looks half-written is a reason to go and read the
reply, not a finding on its own.

Read the reply for which of two things it is doing, because they look alike and
mean opposite things. Treating the message as not having arrived — asking for
it again, saying nothing usable came through — is the first. Engaging with what
the message says and asking which of several things they meant is the second:
it was read, it was understood as an attempt, and it was not enough to act on.
That second one is something they did, it is theirs, and it belongs to the step
that reads the whole task rather than here.

**Where it is not clear, it is input.** The two costs are not equal. A message
wrongly left in goes to the step that reads whole tasks, which finds little in
it, which is close enough to true. A message wrongly picked out is something
they did that no longer exists, and nothing after this can recover it, because
everything after this reads what this step produced. So doubt resolves one way,
and the list stays short.

Nothing is deleted: every message keeps its index and its task, and the ones
named here stay where they are, read as circumstance rather than as something
they did.

## Which messages are about running the work

A third job, asked of every message that is input. The scale reads what a
person says about the thing being made: what it should become, what is true of
what came back, that it has stopped, what they want to be told about it. Some
messages are about how the work is run instead. A message is outside when
acting on it would change only where, by whom or when the work is done, or when
it asks only how far the work has got: whether a step is done, started or
waiting. Those messages are theirs
and they are input, but they say nothing about what the thing should be, so the
scale does not read them. Name each in `outside` with one line saying what
about running the work it is about. It keeps its task and its place.
A question about what the thing is, does or should be is `knowing`, not
outside.

Where the thing being made is itself the kind of thing work runs on — a
machine, an account, a schedule — messages about it are not outside: for that
work, it is the thing being made.

A message that also says anything about what the thing should be, or what is
true of it, is not outside.

**Where it is not clear, it is not outside.** A message wrongly kept in is read
and found to carry little; a message wrongly put outside is gone from every
ladder, and nothing after this step can bring it back.

## Which column each message belongs to

A fourth job, asked of every message that is input and not outside: which one of four columns
it belongs to, told apart by what the message is for.

- `asking` — it is for getting something made, changed, or done.
- `reacting` — it is for saying what is currently true of something that came
  back, whether or not anything was built.
- `blocked` — it is for reporting that progress has stopped.
- `knowing` — it is for being told something they do not yet have, except a
  question only about how far the work has got, which is outside.


What tells `asking` from `knowing` is what would exist once the message is
acted on. A thing that did not exist before, or a change to one, is `asking`.
An answer about what is already so — a fact, a figure, an explanation, whether
something holds — is `knowing`: being handed such an answer is being told it,
however the message is worded.

Whether it is written as a question has no bearing. A proposition put up to be
confirmed or corrected asks to be told whether it holds, and is `knowing`.
What came back decides the rest. When it was an answer — something said rather
than something made — a message that tests it, challenges it, points out a
contradiction in it, or reconciles it with something else asks to be told
whether the answer holds, and is `knowing`. When it was a made thing, saying
what is wrong with it, however precisely and against whatever, is `reacting`,
and saying that work on it has stopped is `blocked`. A message that asks for
something further to be made, changed or done with what came back is `asking`: it says what
should happen next, not what is true of what came back. A verdict that what
came back is not what they meant is `reacting`; `blocked` is for reporting
that the work has stopped going forward: it fails, or cannot get past a point.
A message that asks again for what was already asked, adding nothing, is
`asking`: the same request sent again, not a report that the work has stopped.

One message, one column. Where a message seems to do two of these, give it the
one that is chiefly what the message is for. The messages are read afterwards
by two standards, each placing only the messages given one of its own columns,
so a message given a column here is placed in that column and nowhere else.

A message named in `notInput` or `outside` is given no column, and neither is a
message in the task with no objective: it is about the exchange, not the work.

## What each task would leave behind

A task is one subject they were working on, so each one says what it would
leave behind: `made`, the thing that would exist, or be different, once the task is done.
It is about the task and never about one message inside it — a message that
makes nothing on its own but without which the next part could not be made is
part of that task like any other, and asking after it here would take it away
from the objective it belongs to.

Where a stretch of conversation would leave nothing behind — nothing in it is
to become true and nothing in it is to be told, so what it is about is the
exchange itself and not the work — that is not a task. It is a task of its own
carrying `"objective": null`, with `made` empty.

Being told something is not that case. An answer they did not have before is
something that would be different afterwards, so a stretch whose whole
objective is to be told something is a task like any other, and `made` says
what they would then have. How much there was to say in reply has no bearing on
this.

What is left behind is not only a thing that can be pointed at. A decision
settled, a cause found, something somebody can do for themselves from now on —
each of those is something that would be different afterwards, and each is a
`made`. A task they gave up on partway still says what they were reaching for:
`made` is what the task was for, not what survived it.

Where it is not clear whether anything would be left behind, it is a task and
`made` says what they were after. The two costs are not equal here either. A
task wrongly kept is read by the step that reads whole tasks, which finds
little in it. A task wrongly set aside is work of theirs that no step after
this one can see, and nothing later can put it back.

`made` is always given. An empty string is an answer — that nothing would be
left behind — and leaving the key out is not one.

Output one JSON object:
{
  "tasks": [
    {
      "objective": "what they were trying to get made, in their words where you can",
      "made": "what would exist, or be different, once this task is done; empty when nothing would",
      "messages": [0, 3, 4, 9],
      "reopened": true
    }
  ],
  "columns": [
    {"message": 0, "column": "asking|reacting|blocked|knowing"}
  ],
  "notInput": [
    {"message": 7, "why": "one line: what in the record shows it — for one the reply could not read, what the reply said"}
  ],
  "outside": [
    {"message": 5, "why": "one line: what about running the work it is about"}
  ]
}

`columns` has one entry for every message that is input and belongs to a task
with an objective, and none for a message named in `notInput` or `outside`, or
held in the task with no objective.

`outside` is a list, and an empty one when every message is about the thing
being made.

`notInput` is a list, and an empty one when nothing in this conversation
belongs in it, which is the ordinary case.

`reopened`: whether they returned to this objective after appearing to have
left it — they had moved on to something else, or nothing about it came for a
stretch, and then it started up again. It does not close or open anything; it
is how a task that looks abandoned turns out to have been alive.
